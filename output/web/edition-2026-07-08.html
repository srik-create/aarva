Why did Benjamin Franklin turn to chemistry and botany to make paper money feel real?

We analyzed paper money printed by Ben Franklin to uncover his anti‑counterfeiting techniques and materials innovations

The Conversation ·Khachatur Manukyan, Associate Research Professor of Physics & Astronomy, University of Notre Dame

Writing on 4 June 2026, a team of materials scientists examines how Benjamin Franklin treated the printing press as a laboratory for social trust. While history often frames him as a statesman, this scientific analysis of colonial currency reveals a craftsman focused on the physical properties of ink and paper. By embedding mica and experimenting with graphite, Franklin wasn't just making money; he was engineering a solution to a crisis of confidence. The piece observes how these 18th-century material innovations formed the quiet foundation for the security features found in modern wallets.

A materials science analysis of nearly 600 colonial American bills details the technical innovations Benjamin Franklin used to deter counterfeiting. Microscopic imaging and chemical testing identify his use of graphite-based inks, mica particles, and printed leaf patterns. These physical security features were engineered to establish the public trust necessary for the colonies’ early paper-based economy.
