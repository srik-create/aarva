When scientists cut a strand of DNA, what actually stitches the pieces back together?

The Incredible, Elegant DNA Repair System Makes Gene Editing Work

Quanta Magazine ·Annie Melchor

Biology textbooks usually treat DNA like a pristine blueprint locked safely behind glass. But in a piece published on 23 November 2026, Quanta Magazine frames genetic code as a messy, heavily used recipe book. A single cell suffers tens of thousands of microscopic tears every day just from keeping a body alive. The story sets up a quiet realization about modern gene editing. The medical breakthroughs making headlines don't actually come from the cutting tools scientists invented. They rely entirely on the ancient repair crews our cells already use to survive.

An explanation of why DNA breaks down during everyday cellular activity, and how the body's natural repair systems make modern gene editing possible. Outlines the different types of genetic damage, from single-letter errors to severed strands. Shows how tools like CRISPR do not rewrite the genome directly, but instead make precise cuts that force the cell's own repair pathways to stitch in new material.
