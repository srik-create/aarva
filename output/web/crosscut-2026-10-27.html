Crosscut · why pop music changed

20 min ·narrated by Sulafat/Charon/Vindemiatrix

Turn on a pop station right now, and the songs sound strangely flat, looping the same chords into a pleasant, forgettable hum. Writing earlier this month, Ted Gioia blames the music industry. He argues labels have traded actual songwriting for safe, algorithm-friendly loops, driving bored fans to older hits. But looking at the exact same trend, Spencer Kornhaber sees something else entirely. He suggests listeners actually want that blurry sound, actively seeking out ambient tracks to serve as a quiet, cinematic score for their daily commutes. Placed side by side, these pieces present a real puzzle. Is modern pop stuck in a rut because the industry got lazy, or because audiences simply prefer background noise?

Just last week, 84-year-old Paul McCartney mocked modern pop by improvising a four-chord song during an interview. Ted Gioia uses that moment to show how the music industry traded actual songwriting for safe, repetitive loops designed to please streaming algorithms. Listen for how he connects this fear of musical risk to a larger shift, explaining exactly why bored fans are abandoning new releases to stream fifty-year-old rock anthems instead.

Ted Gioia leaves you staring at a radio dial stuck on repeat, where risk-averse labels and streaming algorithms force artists into the same safe, four-chord loops. It is easy to walk away from his piece blaming executives for killing musical creativity. But read alongside Spencer Kornhaber, up next, that same repetitive flatness takes on a completely different shape. Kornhaber doesn't look at the boardroom. He looks at a traveler sweating in a sad, low-ceilinged hotel gym, wearing wired earbuds. Notice how, for that listener, a blurry, repetitive pop track isn't a failure of songwriting. It is a tool. It turns a miserable session on the rowing machine into a movie scene where they get to be the star. Put the two writers together, and a surprising picture surfaces. Where Gioia sees a business forcing anemic formulas onto bored fans, Kornhaber's piece makes you wonder if those fans are actually driving the demand—actively seeking out ambient music to score the mundane chores of their everyday routines. What looks like a creative dead end from one angle suddenly turns into a highly effective coping mechanism from the other.

Music for the Movie-Brained

The Atlantic·Spencer Kornhaber

Read at source →

A flat, repeating four-chord track plays through a pair of headphones on a crowded morning train. What sits behind that sound is a quiet standoff between the industry and the audience. Record executives are certainly relying on safe, predictable math to keep the streams rolling. Yet millions of listeners are actively reaching for that exact blur to soften the edges of a long workday. If a song is engineered specifically to fade away while someone folds the laundry, is it still art, or has it become just another household appliance?
