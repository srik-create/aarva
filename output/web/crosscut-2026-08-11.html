Connie Chang leaves you picturing a nine-year-old boy smashing a tablet in tears because a video game operates like a Las Vegas casino. The sheer mismatch between a child and a slot machine feels like an unfair fight. Then you turn to Jessica Griffin. She breaks down exactly how humans learn to handle frustration and anger. Put the two together, and something specific jumps out. Griffin explains that emotional regulation takes years of practice and adult help to build. Read alongside Chang, that clinical reality puts those video game tantrums in a sharp new light. Developers are throwing adult-level psychological triggers—gambling loops and manufactured unfairness—at kids who are barely starting to figure out how to calm down when they drop a toy. The meltdowns Chang describes start to look less like bad behavior and more like a simple biological limit. A child's developing brain is completely short-circuiting under a load it was never built to carry.

What lingers is the staggering gap between the relentless pressure of the software and the slow, physical work of growing up.
