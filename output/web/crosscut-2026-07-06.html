Tom Nichols leaves the listener with the image of a foreign policy that feels like a canceled television show—a series of "oddball plot devices" that never add up to a coherent moral story. When the reasons for war shift from regime change to the "will of God" in a single afternoon, the very idea of a national narrative starts to dissolve. It’s a chaotic, top-down view where the script is written by whim. Notice how Simon Garnett, writing from a speculative 2026, approaches this same breakdown of narrative from the opposite direction. While Nichols tracks the whiplash of a leader’s shifting justifications, Garnett suggests that moral clarity doesn't come from a better scriptwriter. It comes from the ground up. Where Nichols sees a "buffet" of explanations, Slavenka Drakulić finds truth in the "hard, cold floor" of a hospital ward or the specific names of the dead. The interesting thing is that both writers are staring at the same void where a stable political story used to be. Nichols watches the grand narrative fail because it’s transactional and hollow; Garnett points to a thinker who abandoned grand narratives entirely to focus on the human details that survive the collapse. It turns the focus from the loud, incoherent promises of a president to the quiet, meticulous reality of those living through the aftermath.

Viewing the present from a speculative future makes the whiplash of transactional politics feel like little more than static. There’s a specific weariness in watching a nation trade its values for a momentary advantage, leaving no clear story behind. Yet, the human details—a shared meal or a writer’s careful observation—have a way of surviving even when the big political structures fall apart. These fragments become the only reliable guides left. If the grand narratives no longer offer a path forward, which small, everyday truths are actually worth holding onto?
