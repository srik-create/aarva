What makes the jump to Jake Lundberg interesting is how he rewinds the clock to 1903, a time when critics actually dismissed tennis as too soft. Back then, sports were supposed to be dangerous. Football was praised specifically because players risked their lives to prove their toughness, while tennis was pitched as a graceful, bloodless alternative. But read alongside Nathan's account of Djokovic’s collapse, Lundberg's history takes on a dark irony. The early promoters who wanted a civilized game might not recognize the grueling war of attrition tennis has become. Put the two pieces together, and the old debate over which sport is the most brutal suddenly looks entirely different. Tennis didn't need tackling to become a test of survival. It just needed players willing to push themselves until they break.

A century ago, promoters sold tennis as a polite afternoon on a pristine lawn, entirely safe from the bruised ribs and bloody noses of contact sports. Today, the violence just happens from the inside out. A champion bends over a baseline towel, heaving from exhaustion, his joints worn down to the bone. The sport kept its quiet crowds and white clothes, but lost the gentle recreation it was built on. If a game designed for grace now requires athletes to break themselves apart to win, what exactly is the audience clapping for?
