There is a curious puzzle in how to coax young adults out of their bedrooms and into the physical world. Two pieces published just days apart offer entirely different solutions to this modern isolation. In Defector, Annie Geng reviews a new comedy taking a surprisingly direct approach: urging a screen-bound generation to risk the messy vulnerability of actual sex. Sophia Nguyen, writing for The Atlantic, reports on the business side of this same problem. Theater owners are betting that if human connection isn't enough to draw twenty-somethings out of the house, maybe heated recliners and self-serve beer walls will. What emerges is a striking contrast in what it actually takes to get a young audience to simply show up.

A lonely generation is trading intimacy for therapy speak and a solid night of sleep. Writing just last week, Annie Geng reviews a new Gregg Araki comedy that calls out this exact retreat from vulnerability. Notice how she contrasts the film's clueless 23-year-old protagonist—a guy who literally has question marks tattooed on his hands—with an older generation's messy, driven libido, turning a movie review into a raunchy plea to risk getting hurt.

Annie Geng leaves you with the image of a 62-year-old director practically begging young adults to get out of bed, risk a little embarrassment, and actually touch somebody. Her piece frames the retreat into phone screens as a crisis of physical connection. Read alongside Geng, Sophia Nguyen’s reporting on the movie business takes that exact same anxiety and turns it into a corporate panic. Nguyen tracks Hollywood’s obsession with cheery statistics about Zoomers returning to the cinema, driven by a very real fear that an entire demographic simply prefers staying inside. What jumps out when you put these two together is the wildly different tactics used to pull young people out of their bedrooms. Gregg Araki uses his films to push for raw, messy vulnerability. But as Nguyen points out, the theater industry is trying to win them back through sheer comfort. Instead of asking young adults to brave the friction of the outside world, theater owners are trying to recreate the coziness of the bedroom—luring them with heated recliners and self-serve beer walls. The shared assumption is hard to miss. The isolation of a screen-addicted generation isn't just a cultural tragedy. It is a massive financial liability for the people trying to sell them a reason to leave the house.

What lingers here is the gap between two very different ways to empty out a teenager's bedroom. A filmmaker begs young adults to drop their screens and risk the messy vulnerability of physical touch. Theater owners just try to coax them out with heated recliners and self-serve beer. One demands emotional friction; the other promises pure comfort. Both want to pull a lonely generation back into the physical world. The question left behind is this: Does wrapping someone in luxury actually help them connect, or does it just move their isolation into a darker room?
