The Smithsonian piece leaves the listener at the finish line in London, watching Josh Kerr shave fractions of a second off a record that stood for nearly thirty years. It’s a story of Project 222 and the giants who came before, framing the mile as a pursuit of something almost superhuman. But behind the blistering speed and the roaring crowds lies a much quieter, more relatable struggle. Eike Buabang, up next, isn't looking at the final lap; the focus shifts to the very first moment of the day—the groggy battle with the snooze button. While the first piece celebrates the peak of human willpower, Buabang suggests that relying on willpower is actually a losing game. The interesting thing is how these two perspectives overlap. Kerr’s record-breaking run looks like a feat of extreme motivation, but read alongside Buabang, it starts to look like the ultimate result of a brain that has simply turned "going" into an automatic reflex. If the mile is sport’s greatest goal, Buabang shows that the engine behind it is a neurological shortcut that closes the gap between wanting to do something and actually doing it. It turns out the elite athlete and the casual jogger are using the exact same hardware to beat the morning chill.

Josh Kerr’s 3:42.66 mile began long before he hit the track, built on the same neurological shift that helps a tired person ignore the snooze button on a rainy Tuesday. Whether it’s a bet on an 18th-century cobblestone street or a solo jog through the park, the body eventually stops asking for permission to move. What lingers is the idea that the hardest part of a run isn't the lungs, but the moment the brain decides to stop arguing. If the body can be trained to move without thinking, how much of talent is actually just a quiet mind?
