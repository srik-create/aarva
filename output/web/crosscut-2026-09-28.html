Look at a map of global rat distribution, and the Canadian province of Alberta is a blank spot. Writing earlier this month, Deena Mousa explains how a region of five million people managed to stay completely rat-free. To keep the rodents out, the government essentially built a permanent, localized surveillance state. Notice how the province capitalized on a narrow eastern corridor to seal the border before the Norway rat could establish a foothold.

Reading Deena Mousa, what jumps out is the image of bureaucracy as a fortress. Alberta keeps the rats at bay by sealing a border and maintaining a stubborn, localized isolation. It is a never-ending, paranoid fight to keep a physical threat out. The next piece looks at another massive public health triumph, but it flips that dynamic completely. Here, the goal is not to block an invasion, but to rush a lifesaving medicine in. Notice how the Zambian medical agency, with just 150 staff members, faces a completely different problem than Alberta’s rat patrol. They lack the manpower to build a massive regulatory wall to test the HIV drug lenacapavir on their own. So, instead of isolation, the piece shows a bureaucracy succeeding through global cooperation. Read alongside Mousa, the Zambian approach looks like the exact opposite kind of success. Where Alberta wins by closing its doors and trusting only its own daily vigilance, Zambia wins by opening up — bypassing its own limits by relying on a chain of trust that stretches far beyond its borders. Both show government systems actually working, but they pull in completely opposite directions.

A border guard checks a truck for rats in Alberta, while a health official in Zambia stamps a drug approval based on a test run in Europe. What lingers from these two places is how differently competence can look. Success might demand fierce, stubborn isolation. Or it might mean admitting local limits and leaning on an invisible chain of global trust. The question left to sit with is practical: when the next crisis hits, how does a government know whether to build a taller fence, or to ask outsiders for help?
