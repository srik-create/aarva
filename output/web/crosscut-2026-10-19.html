The first piece leaves you looking back at the aftermath of the Civil War and Nuremberg, sitting with the heavy cost of letting anti-democratic actors walk away unpunished. It builds a hard, historical case that failing to penalize those who attack the system only guarantees they will try again. But turn the page to Michael John-Hopkins, and the threat model completely flips. Where the first piece worries about a government being too soft on actual threats, John-Hopkins points at the immediate danger of leaders inventing threats to justify a power grab. He looks straight at recent pushes for strict voter ID laws, showing how politicians use the excuse of stopping foreign interference to strip eligible citizens of their right to vote. Read alongside that first historical warning, John-Hopkins’s argument reveals a narrow tightrope for any fragile democracy. You have to defend the system with enough teeth to deter actual criminals, but the moment you give leaders the power to lock down an election in the name of security, they might just use it to lock the voters out. Both pieces take for granted that the system is under attack, but putting them together forces you to watch the front and back doors at the exact same time.

What lingers is a trap built into the law itself. A free society needs teeth. If it refuses to punish the people who break the rules, they just wait for their next chance. But the moment a government flexes its muscle for security, it hands ambitious politicians an excuse to lock the doors. The line between defending a system and strangling it is terrifyingly thin. How does a country build walls strong enough to keep out the people trying to burn it down, without handing a strongman the keys to the gate?
