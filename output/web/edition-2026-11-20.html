Future Gazing

What happens to a farming town when a distant lab learns to manufacture its only crop?

The End Of Extraction

Noema ·Benjamin Siegel

Much of the panic about the future of work focuses on office jobs lost to software. But in this piece from 19 November 2026, Benjamin Siegel asks a quieter question about the people who pull raw materials from the earth and sea. What happens to the world's farmers and fishermen when chemists learn to brew their livelihoods in a lab? As synthetic biology gets better at replacing natural resources like fish oil and coffee beans, Siegel asks how to manage the fallout when entire regions lose the exports that keep them alive.

An examination of how synthetic substitutes displace extracted resources, tracing historical shifts from nineteenth-century dyes to modern opioids. To manage the economic fallout of a coming wave of synthetic biology, the author proposes a system of transition trusts. Funded by taxes on lab-grown alternatives like synthetic coffee or omega-3s, these trusts would support the workers and regions left behind.

Behind the News

How do you teach college students about consent if they never learned basic physical boundaries?

Preventing campus sexual assault could start in kindergarten

Vox ·Lara Bazelon

Every time a sexual assault allegation hits a college campus, administrators usually respond with stricter rules and harsher punishments. Writing for Vox on 14 November 2026, a law professor who defends students in Title IX hearings suggests the system is looking at the problem backward. Instead of waiting until young adults are drunk in a dorm room and entirely lacking in communication skills, the actual work of prevention has to happen years earlier. The essay poses a highly practical question: what would happen if schools taught basic physical boundaries to children before they even learn to read?

An argument for treating campus sexual assault as a problem of early education rather than college discipline. Draws on public health research and the author's experience handling campus assault cases to show how teaching boundary-setting from kindergarten onward reduces later violence. Concludes by reviewing the political resistance that keeps comprehensive sex education out of most American public schools.

A Smart Escape

Why do the most famous scientific discoveries hide behind lucky accidents and burned notebooks?

The trouble with serendipity

Works in Progress ·Saloni Dattani

The story of the open window that led to penicillin makes for a great dinner party tale. Writing on 19 November 2026, Saloni Dattani asks why people prefer these neat myths of accidental genius over the messy reality. Looking at the burned notebooks and bitter rivalries behind the world's biggest breakthroughs, the essay pauses on a quiet question: what gets lost when textbooks credit blind luck instead of grueling, unglamorous work?

An investigation into famous accidental scientific discoveries, from penicillin to the first vaccines, showing how archival records contradict popular stories of chance. Letters and lab notes reveal that these breakthroughs actually came from systematic trial and error rather than sudden luck. The false stories survived because researchers actively obscured their methods to protect commercial monopolies, hide professional rivalries, and simplify years of unglamorous work.
