A leather-bound book of French philosophy and a glass of unpasteurized milk don't have much in common, until one looks at the people selling them. Writing just a few days ago, Geoff Shullenberger observes how radical academic theories—once meant to topple authority—became a polished new establishment. Yesterday, Annie Waldman profiled a mogul building a thirty-million-dollar empire by telling customers to trust their gut over a century of science. What’s striking is how the student of high theory and the raw-milk buyer use the same language of rebellion to feel smarter than the crowd. Both claim to be thinking for themselves, yet they often end up following new leaders into very old traps.

There was a time when carrying a book by Jacques Derrida felt as rebellious as sporting a pink mohawk. Writing for Compact earlier this week, Geoff Shullenberger tracks how these dense French ideas moved from the radical fringe. They eventually became the everyday furniture of university life. Listen for the moment he explains how skepticism—once a tool for outsiders—hardened into a predictable brand for the new academic establishment. Even the most difficult ideas can become routine.

Shullenberger leaves a picture of a graduate student wearing Jacques Derrida like a designer label, using dense philosophy to signal they aren't part of the "docile horde." It’s a specific kind of rebellion where being "critical" is the ultimate way to show you're in the know. But notice what happens when that same spirit of defiance moves from the library to the dairy aisle. Annie Waldman’s profile of Mark McAfee introduces a different kind of nonconformist—one who bypasses the library to challenge 150 years of public health science. What jumps out is how the raw-milk drinker and the philosophy student are essentially reading from the same script. Both see the mainstream as more than just wrong; they see it as a trap for the unthinking. Where the academic uses Lacan to feel smarter than everyone else, McAfee’s customers use unpasteurized milk to feel physically "pure" and politically "awake." Shullenberger shows how skepticism eventually becomes the new establishment, "part of the furniture." Read alongside Waldman, that observation starts to look like a warning. The $30 million raw-milk business suggests that when people trade old experts for new leaders who promise "the truth," they often just end up following a different, more profitable kind of authority.
