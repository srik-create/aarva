Nabeel Kolothumthodi leaves the reader with a sharp image: ordinary people standing outside locked government buildings, shouting to make themselves heard. He frames this noise not as a breakdown of the system, but as the basic operating manual for a republic. A vote happens in a single day, he points out, but accountability has to be forced the rest of the year. The piece up next takes that constitutional theory and drops it directly onto the streets of modern Delhi. Amrita Basu profiles the Cockroach Janta Party, a youth-led movement currently clashing with the government over deleted voter rolls. Notice what happens when you read them back to back. Kolothumthodi argues that questioning bodies like the Election Commission is a necessary tradition dating back to the freedom struggle. Then Basu introduces the Gen Z protesters actually doing it right now, facing down police barricades and detention to demand an independent inquiry. Read alongside Kolothumthodi’s historical view, the young demonstrators in Basu’s article stop looking like a chaotic, internet-fueled fringe. Instead, their memes and street protests start to look exactly like the democratic maintenance work Kolothumthodi says keeps the country alive.

There is a vast distance between the quiet rooms where laws are drafted and the noisy streets where they are tested. What lingers is the reality that a founding text is just paper until someone decides to act it out. The right to dissent might be written in ink, but it takes teenagers armed with smartphones and legal briefs to enforce it. The question left behind is this: When a young generation has to fight this hard to remind a government of its own rules, are they repairing the old system, or preparing to build a new one?
