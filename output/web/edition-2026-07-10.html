The Davis Wing, the B-24 Liberator, and the Self-Made Bet That Paid Off

War on the Rocks ·Mike Benitez

The piece, published on 8 July 2026, uses the history of the B-24 Liberator to ask how innovation actually happens. While the aeronautical establishment of the 1930s relied on a catalog of proven designs, a self-taught freelancer named David Davis looked at falling water droplets and saw a different path. The story follows the friction between credentialed experts and a stubborn outsider, offering a reminder that the most significant technological leaps often come from those who aren't afraid to trust their own eyes over the industry standard.

A history of the Davis wing and its role in the development of the B-24 Liberator, the most produced American military aircraft of World War II. It traces how a self-taught engineer’s empirical design for a high-efficiency airfoil overcame institutional skepticism to provide the bomber with superior range and payload capacity. The account contrasts this intuitive approach with the formal aeronautical research that eventually defined the laminar-flow revolution.
