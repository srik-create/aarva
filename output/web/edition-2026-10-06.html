What happens to a thousand-year-old tree when it gets too hot to breathe?

America&#8217;s tallest, most spectacular trees are getting&#8230;heat stroke?

Vox ·Neel Dhanesha

A Vox piece published on 6 October 2026 tracks a quiet crisis inside America's tallest trees. Coast redwoods are famous for surviving wildfires and living for millennia, but extreme heat exposes a physical vulnerability. When temperatures spike, the trees close their pores to save water, effectively shutting down their ability to take in carbon and feed themselves. With the coastal fog that usually cools them disappearing, the question is no longer just whether these giants will stop growing. It is what happens to the ecosystems suspended in their branches when the trees begin to starve.

A report on how coast redwoods respond to extreme heat, drawing on a two-year study of their leaves. Measurements show that the trees close their pores during temperature spikes to save water, halting photosynthesis and risking carbon starvation. The data outlines how this heat stress and a decline in coastal fog could stunt tree growth and dry out the elevated habitats they support.

How can a doctor tell the exact truth and still inflate a hospital bill?

Opinion: I’m a doctor. Here’s what I want the public to know about AI tools and health care costs

STAT News ·Leah Pierson

Writing in STAT News on 6 October 2026, a physician describes a quiet shift happening inside hospitals. Artificial intelligence is now scanning patient charts to find minor secondary conditions—like a temporary dip in sodium—that allow hospitals to charge insurers more money. The setup puts doctors in a strange position. They face a daily flood of automated emails asking them to document conditions that barely affect patient care, all to satisfy the billing department. It captures exactly what happens when new medical technology is built to optimize revenue instead of patient care.

An account of how hospitals use automated software to scan patient records for secondary conditions, allowing them to charge insurers higher rates. Drawing on the author's experience as a medical resident, the essay outlines the financial standoff between hospitals and insurance companies over these billing codes. It proposes a practical fix for medical software that would let doctors clarify when a lab result actually requires treatment.
