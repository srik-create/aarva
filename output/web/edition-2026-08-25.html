Does the brain see the world as it is, or as the body needs it to be?

A New Framework for How the Brain Compresses Our Noisy World

Quanta Magazine ·Conor Feehly

A sudden sound in a dark alley feels entirely different than the exact same noise on a sunny street. In a Quanta Magazine article published on 24 August 2026, that everyday shift frames a strange new theory about human perception. Neuroscience usually treats the brain like a clerk at a filing cabinet, sorting sights and sounds after they happen. The framework presented here proposes the opposite. It suggests the brain projects categories onto the physical world before a person even registers them, driven entirely by the body's basic need to survive.

A proposed model of brain function arguing that the nervous system does not passively sort sights and sounds into fixed mental files. Instead, the brain actively builds categories based on the body's immediate energy and survival needs. Using anatomical and brain-imaging data, the authors map how this predictive sorting works across the entire brain rather than in one specific region.

What happens to a country's sanctions when the rest of the world stops taking them seriously?

The U.S. Government Must Preserve the Soft Power of Its Sanctions

Just Security ·Anton Moiseienko

When a government freezes a corrupt official's bank account, the legal block is only half the punishment. The real sting relies on a chain reaction of global banks and allied nations voluntarily cutting ties. An essay published in Just Security on 19 August 2026 asks what happens when the sanctioning country loses its credibility. If an administration starts using economic penalties for political vendettas, it doesn't just anger its allies. It risks breaking the unwritten trust that makes those penalties work in the first place.

An analysis of how targeted government sanctions rely on international credibility to be effective. The author details the mechanics of anti-corruption sanctions, showing that their real force comes from voluntary cooperation by foreign governments and global banks rather than strict legal rules. Using these financial tools for political ends, the author argues, destroys this secondary support and weakens a primary method for punishing international corruption.
