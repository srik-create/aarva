In the mid-1800s, the scientific world finally accepted that life doesn't simply materialize from dust; it requires a physical parent to exist. Sarah Waring, writing for Eurozine just days ago, revisits the experiments that overturned the theory of spontaneous generation. It is worth pausing on how this history sits next to David Dayen’s piece from earlier this month in The American Prospect. Dayen observes a quiet collapse in the surveillance systems designed to track and stop the spread of biological threats through the food chain. There is a strange dissonance in knowing exactly how a parasite moves, yet choosing to look away until the bill arrives at the grocery store.

David Dayen, writing earlier this month, connects the rising cost of a hamburger to the return of a flesh-eating parasite in Texas. The piece argues that political disinvestment in "funny-sounding" government programs has real consequences. Dayen leaves the listener with the image of a firebreak in the Panamanian rainforest that has finally buckled. The return of the screwworm to Texas represents more than a failure of border policy; it marks the collapse of a sixty-year effort to use sterilization as a wall against a parasitic life cycle. Notice how Dayen treats the fly’s return as a consequence of human neglect, a predictable result of letting a mechanical defense system rust. Turning to Sarah Waring, the perspective shifts from the cost of beef to the very nature of how life moves. Waring revisits a time when scientists actually believed parasites like these could emerge from thin air—the recipe for mice or the spontaneous generation of lice from excrement. Read alongside Dayen, the historical debate between Pasteur and Pouchet feels less like a dusty academic dispute and more like the blueprint for modern biosecurity. If Pasteur hadn't proved that life requires a progenitor, the sterile fly program would never have existed. The discovery that life cannot be conjured from a soiled shirt and some wheat grains was supposed to give us total control. But as Dayen shows, that control only lasts as long as the infrastructure supporting it.

Pasteur’s swan-neck flasks proved that life requires a physical path and a progenitor. Today, as surveillance budgets shrink and parasites find new gaps in the shipping and transit infrastructure, that nineteenth-century victory feels fragile. Understanding how a pathogen travels is only half the battle. What’s left to sit with is the widening gap between biological certainty and political indifference. If the mechanics of contagion are no longer a mystery, why does the collective will to intercept them continue to falter?
