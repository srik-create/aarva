Crosscut · ai for life decisions

22 min ·narrated by Sulafat/Charon/Vindemiatrix

Right now, teenagers across China are typing prompts into chatbots, asking algorithms to pick their college majors. Human tutors simply cost too much, so students are handing their futures over to software. Viola Zhou captures this shift in a piece for Rest of World. Putting her reporting next to an essay by Pawan Jain creates a sharp tension. Jain proposes a strict rule for personal finance: bots are fine for basic math, but dangerous for irreversible choices. Reading these two pieces together forces a pause. There is a massive difference between calculating compound interest and deciding the entire course of a career, yet people are increasingly crossing that line, letting software dictate their most permanent decisions.

After taking China’s grueling college entrance exam, teenagers have just three weeks to lock in a major they can almost never change. In a piece published this week, Viola Zhou watches these students hand their futures over to algorithms, since private human coaches cost too much. Listen for the exact details one 18-year-old types into a chatbot—her test score, a moderate tuition budget, and a wish to live near the coast—hoping to find a safe career path.

Watch an eighteen-year-old type her test scores into a chat window, and the stakes are hard to ignore. Viola Zhou leaves you looking at students handing the trajectory of their careers over to an algorithm, simply because a human coach costs too much. Turn the page to Pawan Jain, and that exact trade-off gets a sharp set of boundaries. Jain looks at people who can only afford free advice and draws a strict line. He points out that algorithms are perfectly safe for basic math—like figuring out credit card interest or sizing up an emergency fund. But he warns against trusting them with irreversible choices. Read alongside Zhou, Jain’s financial rule suddenly looks like a warning label for those teenagers. The students in China are doing exactly what Jain advises against: making a permanent, one-shot life decision based on a bot's output. The interesting thing is how both writers trace this back to the exact same problem. Whether someone is picking a college major or planning a retirement, human expertise is expensive. When people are priced out of a professional opinion, a confident chatbot starts to look like the only option left.

A finance professor can afford to draw a hard line against letting algorithms make irreversible choices. A teenager staring at a blank college application, priced out of human advice, faces a colder reality. What lingers from these two stories is the gap between safe rules and economic necessity. A chatbot might be a terrible tool for picking a life path. Yet for millions, it is the only counselor in the room. The question left behind is this: when human guidance becomes a luxury, is a flawed machine better than walking into the dark alone?
