When a country tries to bury its past, where does the need for a homeland actually go?

Return Of The Repressed In Germany

Noema ·Nathan Gardels

Thirty years ago, a small group of writers warned that Germany was ignoring a basic human need for belonging. Writing on 2 October 2026, Nathan Gardels returns to those forgotten warnings as nationalist parties gain ground across the country. He pauses on a difficult question about modern borders: what happens when mainstream politics treats the desire for a homeland as a taboo? When governments dismiss the emotional pull of local community, they leave a void that extremist movements are more than happy to fill.

A historical analysis of how post-war Germany's rejection of national identity created a void that helped fuel current right-wing extremism. Drawing on 30-year-old warnings from German intellectuals, the argument uses this history to rethink Western immigration policy. It concludes with four practical steps for democracies to secure borders, redefine asylum, and integrate newcomers while acknowledging the human need for belonging.

What actually makes a house feel safe in a city you barely know?

People have to be treated as people

Africa Is a Country ·Aidan Huang

Writing on 24 September 2026, a young researcher describes arriving in Chad and renting a room from a fellow expat. She just wants a quiet place to sleep and work. Instead, she watches the household slowly break apart. The friction starts over shared watermelons and missing eggs, then spirals into cut electricity and police summons. The story isolates a very specific kind of failure: what happens when someone tries to substitute money and control for basic human trust.

A researcher's account of a domestic conflict inside a shared house in N’Djamena, Chad. The narrative follows the breakdown of trust between a Chinese businessman, a Chadian landlord, and local household workers over unpaid bills and missing food. Through these everyday disputes, the author shows how daily stability depends on basic respect rather than financial transactions.
