Why would a pigeon need its liver to find the way home on a cloudy day?

How Do Pigeons Find Their Way Home? New Research Suggests the Birds' Remarkable Navigational Skills Come From Their Livers

Smithsonian Magazine

A homing pigeon released twelve miles from its loft usually streaks back as if the devil is behind it. Writing on 2 June 2026, the Smithsonian follows a study suggesting this internal map isn't in the bird's brain or beak, but its liver. By tracking birds with GPS, researchers found that iron-rich immune cells act as a backup compass when clouds hide the sun. The finding offers a physical answer to a biological puzzle that has remained unsolved for over a hundred years.

Homing pigeons may use iron-rich immune cells in their livers as a compass to detect the Earth’s magnetic field. Researchers tracked birds with these cells disabled and found they could not find their way home on cloudy days when the sun was hidden. The findings offer a biological explanation for how birds stay on course without visual cues, though some experts remain skeptical.

Could the random jiggle of atoms be used to solve problems instead of causing errors?

Thermodynamic Computers Go With the (Energy) Flow

Quanta Magazine ·Philip Ball

Standard chips treat heat and random atomic jiggling as a threat to accuracy, spending massive energy to drown out the noise. Physicists featured in this 15 July 2026 report want to flip that logic. By letting circuits mimic the way a protein folds or a cell processes signals, they hope to turn thermal fluctuations into a computational engine. It is a shift from fighting the laws of physics to riding them, potentially making artificial intelligence thousands of times more efficient.

Thermodynamic computing uses random heat fluctuations to perform calculations instead of trying to suppress them. By building circuits that follow the natural flow of energy, researchers are developing systems that can solve mathematical problems and run artificial intelligence using very little power. Recent experiments show these devices can mimic biological processes like protein folding to find answers more efficiently than standard digital computers.
