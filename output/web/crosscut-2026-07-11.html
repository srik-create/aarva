At Least You’re Not Playing in the World Cup Right Now

The Atlantic·Nancy Walecki

A soccer player swallowing a biometric tablet the size of a large vitamin just to survive a ninety-minute match is a reminder of the biological ceiling. Nancy Walecki describes elite athletes treated like high-performance machines, using every gadget available to keep the engine from seizing up in the heat. It leaves the impression that the human body is becoming something to be managed and optimized against a world that is increasingly inhospitable. The interesting thing is how Arianna Huffington shifts the focus from the physical to the mental. Where Walecki describes using technology to shore up physical weaknesses, Huffington suggests that being outmatched by technology might actually be a relief. She points to the way Silicon Valley treats AI as a kind of secular god and argues that if a machine can handle the raw data and the "intelligence" part of life, it frees people to focus on things a sensor can't track—like wisdom, mystery, or spiritual growth. Walecki’s athletes are fighting to stay relevant in a world that’s too hot; Huffington is asking if people have spent too long trying to be the smartest things in the room, only to find that human value might lie somewhere else entirely.

Soccer players strapped into cooling vests show how hard the body fights to stay in the game as the thermometer climbs. It’s a struggle of sweat and sensors against a warming sky. Meanwhile, as silicon chips outpace human logic, the old habit of judging a person’s worth by their brainpower starts to fail. What lingers is a world where the things people do best aren't about winning races or solving equations. When machines do the thinking and the weather pushes the body to its limit, what is left for a person to do that a sensor cannot measure?
