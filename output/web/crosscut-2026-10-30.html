Crosscut · halloween fears and dangers

16 min ·narrated by Sulafat/Charon/Vindemiatrix

Every October, front porches fill up with plastic skeletons and fake spiderwebs. It's a season dedicated to fear. Writing in *The Conversation* just days before Halloween, one essay looks at the witches and ghouls in children's books, pointing out how fictional monsters give kids a safe space to practice bravery. But something interesting emerges when that idea meets a piece published this morning in *Vox*. Away from the ghost stories, the physical layout of an American street holds a much quieter, deadlier threat for a child walking in the dark. Read together, these perspectives draw a sharp line between the imaginary terrors adults invent to build courage, and the very real metal machines parked right in the driveway.

When a mother complained that *Where the Wild Things Are* terrified her toddler, Maurice Sendak had a blunt response: read him something else. Writing last week, Liz Rosenberg, Professor of English, General Literature and Rhetoric, Binghamton University, State University of New York, argues that scary stories let children practice bravery in a safe environment. Listen for how she points to Frog and Toad scaring themselves to show exactly when a little manufactured fear becomes fun.

Liz Rosenberg leaves you thinking about fear as a controlled experiment. Stories about ghouls and the old dark Frog give kids a safe sandbox to practice bravery. It is a comforting idea: adults can curate the exact right dose of spooky to help a child grow. But read alongside Muizz Akhtar, up next, that careful curation starts to look a little ironic. Akhtar points out that while parents worry about fictional monsters or mythical tainted candy, they walk children right into the deadliest night of the year for pedestrians. The two writers are looking at completely different things. Rosenberg looks at the imagination; Akhtar looks at federal crash records. Put them together, though, and a strange contradiction surfaces. Adults spend October carefully managing the imaginary threats kids face in books, making sure a story isn't too intense. Meanwhile, as Akhtar points out, those same adults accept the physical threat of cars as just a normal part of the holiday. Reading Akhtar right after Rosenberg makes you wonder why people fret so much over the emotional impact of a picture book, while shrugging off the lethal danger built right into the street.

Forget tainted candy: The scariest thing on Halloween is parked in your driveway

A child reading a ghost story by flashlight is practicing how to be brave. But when that same child puts on a plastic mask and steps off the curb, the ghosts are replaced by speeding sedans. What remains is the stark gap between the fears adults invent to teach resilience, and the everyday hazards paved into the neighborhood. It leaves a quiet question to sit with. Why is it so simple to guide kids through stories about witches, yet so difficult to build a street where they can walk safely in the dark?
