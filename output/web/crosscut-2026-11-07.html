In an essay published just days ago, Jessica Riskin revisits the French naturalist Jean-Baptiste Lamarck and his widely mocked theory about giraffes stretching their necks for high leaves. Jessica Riskin ends her essay by asking you to look at a giraffe stretching for a branch and see a creature taking charge of its own future. Instead of treating animals as passive clay shaped by random genetic accidents, she argues they are active participants making choices that rewrite their biology. That sweeping historical argument makes the pivot to the next piece unexpectedly fun. The writer of this short Smithsonian Magazine report isn't trying to rewrite the philosophy of science. They are just explaining a highly specific survival tactic: hawkmoths rubbing their genitals together to jam bat sonar. But read alongside Riskin, this brief news story starts to look like a perfect test case for her theory. The piece notes that moths likely started making these sounds to attract mates, and only later co-opted the noise to confuse swooping bats. The standard biological view treats that shift as a lucky, random mutation. Yet with Riskin's defense of Jean-Baptiste Lamarck still fresh in your mind, it's hard not to look at these moths and see exactly what she is talking about: living things actively inventing a new way to survive.

A moth twisting in the dark to dodge a bat might look like a desperate reflex. But that flinching movement belongs to a much longer story. What lingers here is a shift in perspective. Evolution stops looking like a blind lottery of genetic mutations. Animals become active participants in their survival, shaping their biology over thousands of years through the daily grind of staying alive. The question left behind is this: if living things help author their physical future, how much of evolution is driven by random chance, and how much by the simple will to survive the night?
