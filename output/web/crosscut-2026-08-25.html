Out in a deep-red stretch of Iowa, a state auditor keeps winning elections by leaving his partisan labels at the door to talk about quiet competence. Writing for The Atlantic this August, Elaine Godfrey suggests this focus on efficiency might offer a survival guide. But just days later in The American Prospect, Harold Meyerson points in the opposite direction. Pulling from mid-century history, Meyerson argues that abandoning aggressive class conflict for polite technocracy was a historical mistake. Placing these two pieces together reveals a sharp split over how to build political power. There is a real puzzle here about what works better: turning the volume down to fix the plumbing, or turning it up to pick a fight.

Elaine Godfrey leaves the listener with the image of Rob Sand pedaling across Iowa, dodging the Democratic label as if it were a pothole. His strategy for survival in deep-red territory is to strip away the partisan fight entirely. He offers quiet, cheerful competence instead, taking down party signs and focusing on the math of state audits. It looks like a practical survival guide for a hostile political climate. But turning to Harold Meyerson, that exact approach takes on a completely different weight. Meyerson reaches back to the mid-century journals of Arthur Schlesinger to track how the party originally traded the bare-knuckle class politics of the New Deal for the understated, anti-rhetorical mood of the technocrat. Notice what happens when these two visions sit side by side. Both writers are looking at the exact same instinct—the political urge to drop the ideological fight and just focus on making things work. Read alone, Godfrey’s profile makes Sand’s anti-partisan optimism look like a fresh Midwestern rebellion. Read alongside Meyerson, that same cheerful retreat from the party brand looks less like a new tactic and more like the culmination of a decades-long historical mistake. Godfrey sees the only way a politician survives today. Meyerson sees the exact habit that cost the party its soul.

What lingers after looking at these two maps is a stark choice about how to hold political ground. One approach demands dropping the party labels and simply making the math work for everyday people. The other insists on picking a loud fight and drawing a hard line in the dirt. Both strategies claim to offer the only real way forward. But as the next election approaches, a simple question remains. Do voters ultimately want a leader who fixes the plumbing, or someone who leads the strike?
