In 10th-century Baghdad, poets didn't just eat; they composed verses about the steam from lamb stew. Neha Vermani, writing for Scroll.in in late June, looks back at how these culinary rhymes served as sharp tools for social commentary, a far cry from the hollow imagery of modern AI-generated media. There’s a similar sense of lost mastery in Sara Herschander’s piece for Vox from yesterday. It tracks how the rush toward air conditioning stripped American homes of the porches and thick walls that once kept people safe without a power grid. It’s worth pausing on the trade-off. In the pursuit of instant comfort, some of the most durable ways of living have been quietly set aside.

Those surreal AI videos of talking tacos and cheating strawberries might seem new, but in an essay from late June, Neha Vermani finds their imaginative ancestors in medieval poetry. From Cairo to Shiraz, fruits and vegetables once served as political rivals and wise counselors. Notice her use of a 17th-century debate between a boastful gourd and a patient pine tree to show how food could carry a sharp social critique.

Neha Vermani leaves the listener with the image of a wise old pine tree telling a fast-growing gourd to wait for the winter. It’s a reminder that sophistication isn't just about complexity; it’s about the ability to endure. While Vermani focuses on the "brain-rot" of AI-generated media replacing the rich, symbolic depth of Persian and Arabic poetry, the underlying anxiety is about what happens when culture trades long-term meaning for instant, superficial gratification. The interesting thing is how this same tension between immediate comfort and structural endurance shows up in the physical world. Read alongside Vermani, Sara Herschander’s reporting on heat waves looks less like a story about air conditioning and more like a story about the loss of architectural memory. Herschander points out that by leaning entirely on the AC, American buildings have become "ectotherms"—lizards that can’t regulate their own temperature. Notice how the glass office towers Herschander describes mirror the hollow food dramas Vermani critiques; both are engineered for a specific, controlled environment but lack the built-in logic of a Greek island’s white paint or Iran’s wind catchers. Putting these two together suggests a pattern: by solving for the immediate problem—boredom or heat—the modern approach has created a new kind of fragility that only reveals itself when the system breaks.

What lingers is the quiet sturdiness of a poem that doubles as a survival guide or a wall designed to catch a breeze. Older forms solved immediate problems while carrying a specific weight of history. Reliance on a cooling unit or an automated script offers a shortcut that feels like freedom until the grid fails or the meaning thins out. It’s a shift from systems that endure to those that merely function. If the knowledge of how to live without a switch is lost, what remains to keep life steady when the power stops?
