The Paris Review Daily ·Jordan Castro

This piece from 11 June 2026 describes the career of Chelsey Minnis as a twenty-five-year project of dismantling the notion that poetry must be a quiet, "authentic" overflow of feeling. It considers why a "frying pan full of diamonds" or a "crotch sparkle" might be a more honest way to capture experience than the traditional pursuit of sincerity. By leaning into the aesthetics of noir and the performance of glamour, Minnis suggests that the world is often most visible when it is most stylized.

A critical survey of Chelsey Minnis’s poetry and its career-long rejection of emotional sincerity, tracing her stylistic evolution from the linguistic tropes of Zirconia to the operatic melodrama of her latest collection, Opera Fever. Draws comparisons to the aesthetic artifice of film noir to show how irony, luxury, and cinematic language construct a world where feeling is mediated through performance rather than direct expression.

How does a tiny blue octopus wait a decade in a jar before finally receiving a name?

'Cute Little Guy': Scientists Discover a Tiny Blue Species of Octopus by the Galápagos Islands

Smithsonian Magazine

This Smithsonian Magazine piece, published on 9 June 2026, follows the decade-long journey from a chance encounter at sea to a formal scientific entry. Confirming this palm-sized creature as a new species required years of patience and non-invasive 3D scanning to protect its fragile specimen. The story moves beyond the initial charm of the find to consider the immense difficulty of studying life in the deep. It serves as a quiet reminder that the ocean floor holds secrets that take more than just a camera to truly see.

The formal identification of Microeledone galapagensis, a palm-sized octopus species discovered nearly a decade ago at a depth of 5,800 feet near the Galápagos Islands. Researchers used micro-CT scanning to examine the fragile specimen without dissection, revealing unique anatomical traits such as reverse countershading and a single tooth. These findings illustrate the application of non-invasive imaging in documenting biodiversity within under-explored deep-sea ecosystems.
