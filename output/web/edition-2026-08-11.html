For decades, astronomers have scanned the stars for radio signals and found absolute silence. In this Guardian long read published on 11 August 2026, the absence of aliens becomes a mirror for humanity. The famous Fermi paradox—the question of why the cosmos feels so empty—pushes science directly into philosophy. If the universe is unfathomably vast, Earth's total isolation suggests the leap from stardust to intelligent life is a vanishingly rare accident. Ultimately, the search for extraterrestrials turns out to be a quiet reflection on the fragile improbability of human life.

An account of the scientific search for extraterrestrial life, from the origins of radio astronomy to modern exoplanet discovery. Using the Drake equation to frame the problem, it explains why decades of observation have produced no signs of alien civilizations. It outlines theories for this cosmic silence, showing how our assumptions about extraterrestrials reflect our own biological and technological history.

The piece names that invisible threshold where a book suddenly turns from an obligation into an obsession, asking what might change if readers were actually taught to expect the boredom before the breakthrough.

How does an entire prehistoric food chain end up swallowed whole and turned to stone?

Would You Eat This Cretaceous Turducken?

Defector ·Sabrina Imbler

A 7 August 2026 essay in Defector pauses on a recent paleontological discovery in Australia to ask a surprisingly practical question about ancient food chains. Humans treat stuffing one animal inside another as a bizarre culinary stunt, but in the Cretaceous ocean, it was simply a way of life. A fossil containing three nested levels of predator and prey becomes the starting point to reconstruct a single, chaotic afternoon where everyone was eating someone else. It is a funny, slightly gross reminder of how quickly an apex predator could become lunch.

An account of a 100-million-year-old fossil from Queensland that preserves a three-level prehistoric food chain. The remains show a marine reptile that swallowed a flying reptile, which had just eaten early squid, before being crushed by a larger predator. The discovery offers rare physical evidence of nested Cretaceous predators, explained through the culinary concept of a turducken.
