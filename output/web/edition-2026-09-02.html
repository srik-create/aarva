Future Gazing

If nobody has to work, how do you earn respect without relying on who you know?

After Work, We’ll Have Each Other

Asterisk

Eighteenth-century French aristocrats and 1980s Midwestern society ladies shared one trait: they didn't have to work. In an essay published on 1 September 2026, Asterisk uses these groups to test a popular prediction about artificial intelligence. If machines eventually take over the labor market and leave us with an economy based entirely on human relationships, what happens to society? The historical record suggests that when people cannot earn independent respect through labor, they become clannish and obsessed with status. The essay asks a quiet question about the post-work utopia: who will we become without jobs to ground us?

An examination of how human behavior changes when work is no longer the primary source of status. Compares 18th-century French aristocrats, 1980s Midwestern socialites, and 1950s Bostonian peer groups to show how communities without formal labor become clannish and obsessed with hierarchy. Suggests that modern employment provides an impersonal foundation for respect that makes individual freedom possible.

Humans & Humanity

What happens to your mind when a surgeon cuts out the physical source of fear?

Absurd Adventure And Amygdalectomy Advocacy

Astral Codex Ten ·Scott Alexander

In a 2 September 2026 piece for Astral Codex Ten, Daniel Böttger documents an accidental experiment in human biology. After surgeons removed part of his brain to treat cancer—specifically, his right amygdala—he woke up completely incapable of feeling fear or depression. The account poses a radical question for modern psychiatry. If removing a small piece of tissue permanently clears a lifelong mood disorder without destroying a person's character, why does the medical establishment still treat mental illness entirely as a chemical or psychological problem?

A first-person account of surviving brain cancer and the psychological effects of losing a right amygdala during surgery. The author details how the procedure eliminated his fear response and chronic depression, leaving him unusually cheerful through months of severe medical complications. Drawing on his recovery and background in neuroscience, he argues for using targeted brain surgery to treat severe mental illness.

An essay on James Cameron's digital alterations to Terminator 2 for its 4K rerelease.
