Deep in the Belize jungle, scientists are firing lasers through the canopy to map ancient Maya cities, uncovering evidence of egalitarian societies that Spanish missionaries tried to erase. Across the ocean, Caribbean leaders are making a very different demand of the British Crown, pushing past vague royal apologies to seek financial reparations for transatlantic slavery. Putting these two recent articles side by side shows two distinct ways of confronting a colonial past. One relies on physical digging to correct an indigenous history buried under dirt and myth. The other watches modern nations use international law to hand a monarchy a concrete bill for damages.

Published last week in Smithsonian Magazine, this piece drops right into a remote research camp in Belize, where archaeologists sleep in zinc-roofed shacks and carry machetes to fend off pit vipers. The writer tracks how decades of digging at a site called Caracol prove the ancient Maya built highly complex, egalitarian cities. Notice how the article pins centuries of modern misunderstanding on a single 16th-century Spanish missionary who burned almost all the Maya's written records.

The Smithsonian piece leaves you standing in the Belize jungle, watching archaeologists swing machetes and dig through the dirt to correct a 400-year-old lie. It frames the damage of empire as an erasure—a loss of history that people have to slowly, physically recover. Brooke N. Newman, up next, takes a very different approach to confronting a colonial past. Her piece leaves the jungle and heads straight for Buckingham Palace. Instead of digging up ancient cities to recover a stolen heritage, she points to royal charters and naval records to prove the British monarchy was complicit in the slave trade. The interesting thing is what happens when you put these two methods side by side. Both try to fix a broken historical record. But the first piece focuses on the victims of empire, recovering the truth of the people who were conquered. Newman flips the camera to the perpetrators. Read alongside those physical excavations in Belize, Newman’s argument about King Charles and slavery reparations feels like its own kind of unearthing. It suggests that sometimes correcting history means finding the lost cities, and sometimes it means finding the receipts.

Lasers scanning a jungle canopy and lawyers tallying up centuries of debt are two very different tools for fixing the past. In Belize, it takes decades of sweat just to prove an ancient city was whole. Across the Caribbean, it takes ledgers to calculate the exact price of lives stolen by a monarchy. Both force a colonial power to look at the damage it caused. What's left to sit with is what happens next. Once the truth is finally dug out of the dirt, is an apology ever enough, or does someone have to pay?
