18 min ·narrated by Sulafat/Charon/Vindemiatrix

A quiet argument over why people read lives in the space between a heavy college syllabus and a sandy beach towel. In a recent essay, a professor warns that students are losing the stamina to finish difficult texts, arguing that reading should be a mental workout that demands discipline. But put that warning next to a new history of the beach read from Smithsonian Magazine, and something interesting emerges. For over a century, critics have panicked whenever readers pick up light, entertaining paperbacks simply to relax. Looking at these two perspectives together reveals an old friction between treating a book as rigorous intellectual labor and treating it as pure escape.

Writing late this July, Austin Sarat, William Nelson Cromwell Professor of Jurisprudence and Political Science, Amherst College, describes a quiet shift happening during office hours: students are asking for strategies to skim. Notice how he frames this aversion to finishing whole books. The argument isn't just about nostalgia for heavy reading lists. It is about the cognitive workout lost when students only consume short excerpts instead of wrestling with a difficult text.

But turning to the second piece, an unbylined history of the beach read, that modern crisis starts to look like a very old argument. The second piece travels back to the 1870s, where Brooklyn preachers were already warning vacationers not to pack cheap paperbacks in their trunks, calling light summer novels a danger to the immortal soul. Read alongside this history, Sarat’s frustration takes on a different shape. The two pieces look at entirely different centuries, but putting them together reveals a stubborn friction that has never really gone away. It is the quiet, ongoing fight over what a book is actually supposed to do—whether reading is a rigorous cognitive workout meant to build the mind, or an easy escape meant to give it a rest.
