Crosscut · saving oral traditions

20 min ·narrated by Sulafat/Charon/Vindemiatrix

A singer in ancient Greece carried a melody that changed every time he opened his mouth. Writing earlier this month for The Honest Broker, Ted Gioia says the Odyssey is a jazz session, something meant to be heard. A similar effort shows up in a piece published just today by Furaha Ruguru for Africa Is a Country. While Gioia looks to revive the spirit of Greek epics, Ruguru follows African musicians using digital tools to pull their own ancestral songs out of cold storage in Europe. Something interesting emerges here: a culture’s memory might live in the return of the living voice rather than in a silent archive.

In an essay from last week, Ted Gioia looks past the leather-bound prestige of the Odyssey to find the pulse of a mass-market pulp story. Writing as a new film version hits theaters, he suggests that Homer functioned less like a stiff poet and more like the Stan Lee of antiquity. Notice how he treats the epic as a live performance—more like a jazz set than a silent book—that belongs to the crowd, not the classroom.

What You Won't Learn About the Odyssey from a Movie

The Honest Broker·Ted Gioia

Ted Gioia leaves the listener with the image of a performer sweating under the lights, improvising like a jazz musician to keep a crowd hooked. The point is that the *Odyssey* only breathes when it’s spoken aloud. A story trapped on a silent page is a story that has lost its pulse. This makes Furaha Ruguru’s report from Nairobi feel even more urgent. While Gioia wants to rescue an ancient Greek singer from the quiet of a library, Ruguru is looking at thousands of African musicians whose voices are literally locked in boxes in Germany and Belgium. Notice how both writers treat sound as something that can be stolen or lost when it’s turned into an object for experts to study. Gioia argues that elite translators kill the spirit of a story by making it too polite. Ruguru shows how colonial researchers took the physical recordings themselves, turning living culture into a catalog that the original communities can't even hear. The interesting thing is that digital tools, which often get blamed for killing old traditions, are the very things Ruguru’s subjects use to pick the locks of those European museums. In Nairobi, a laptop isn't just a gadget; it's a way to turn a stored artifact back into a live song.

From colonial storage to open access

Africa Is a Country·Furaha Ruguru

A stack of paper or a hard drive in a vault stays silent until someone sings the notes. An ancient Greek melody and a beat pulled from a museum basement only matter when they're heard. There’s a friction in trying to hold onto the past; sometimes the act of locking a song away actually kills it. Keeping a story alive seems to require a bit of noise and a little mess. When a culture finally gets its voice back, how much of the original song should stay the same, and how much must change to survive?
