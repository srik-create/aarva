When the temperature rises, the expected threat is water creeping up the beach. But the secondary shockwaves of a warming planet are often stranger. What's striking is how these unexpected collapses take entirely different forms. Writing in *The Atlantic*, Christian Elliott watches the physical earth give way, tracking how melting permafrost and shifting water weight cause entire mountains to literally crumble and slide. Then, in *The Diplomat*, Olya K-Mehri looks at a quieter erosion in Tuvalu. Long before the ocean swallows the island, a well-meaning climate migration lottery with Australia is quietly draining the nation of the nurses and teachers it needs to function.

When Australia offered a climate migration lottery to Tuvalu, it looked like a humane escape route from rising seas. But writing in late July, Olya K-Mehri shows how the deal is quietly draining the island of the nurses and teachers it needs to function. Notice the brutal math K-Mehri points out: in a country of just 10,000 people, losing a single randomly selected dentist can wipe out a huge chunk of an entire profession.

Olya K-Mehri leaves you with the quiet image of a Tuvaluan hospital missing a nurse, or a school missing a teacher, all because of a well-meaning lottery ticket. The fragility of a tiny island nation is exposed not by rising water, but by the escape route designed to save its people. Turning to Christian Elliott, that same sense of unexpected collapse scales up to the physical earth. Elliott isn't looking at civil service vacancies. He is looking at the literal ground giving way. Read alongside K-Mehri, Elliott’s reporting on crumbling mountains in Greenland and Alaska feels like a brutal physical echo of Tuvalu’s institutional drain. Notice how both writers look past the obvious threats of a warming planet to spot the secondary shockwaves. K-Mehri shows how a climate migration policy can accidentally empty a country of the people it needs to function. Elliott reveals how melting permafrost is ungluing the bedrock of the Earth, triggering 660-foot tsunami waves from collapsing cliffs. The shared realization is unnerving. Structures long assumed to be permanent—whether a national workforce or a mountain range—are already coming apart in ways nobody prepared for.

What lingers after these stories is the sound of bedrock cracking, or the quiet of an empty island clinic. A warming planet brings the sudden loss of things assumed to be permanent. Both the physical earth and the daily routines of a community can fracture under the weight of invisible shifts. The question left sitting in the dust of these changes isn't about global carbon policy. It is something much closer to home. When the foundations finally give way, how do people decide what to save, and what to leave behind?
