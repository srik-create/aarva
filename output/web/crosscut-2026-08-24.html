Joshua Keating leaves you looking at a North Korea that simply waited Washington out. By trading weapons to Russia and cementing its nuclear program, Kim Jong Un made American isolation irrelevant. Turning to Robert Kagan’s piece on Iran, you watch a similar survival act play out, but with a much sharper edge. North Korea secured its status by building a bomb and ignoring the outside world. Kagan shows Iran doing something entirely different: surviving direct American and Israeli military strikes by holding the global energy supply hostage. Notice how both writers expose the exact same limit to American power. Keating points out that North Korea’s new wealth makes diplomatic overtures useless. Kagan takes that premise a step further. He argues that when Iran attacked Qatar's gas facilities, it proved it could crash the global economy at any moment—forcing the United States to blink first and call off its strikes. Put these two situations together, and the picture changes. You stop seeing isolated states struggling to survive American pressure, and start seeing adversaries who realize Washington has finally run out of moves.
