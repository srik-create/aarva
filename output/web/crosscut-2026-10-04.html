Picture the human brain not as a single machine, but as two distinct organs shoved into one skull. The Smithsonian piece leaves you with a clear boundary line. The back of the brain handles primitive, automatic chores like breathing and swallowing, while the front tackles abstract thought and language. It feels like a neat division of labor between the body’s basic survival instincts and the mind’s high-level logic. But reading Brandon Yip next makes that boundary look a lot blurrier. Yip takes a reaction that belongs squarely in that primitive, automatic camp—the physical urge to gag or recoil in disgust—and argues it is actually doing complex moral work. Some philosophers dismiss disgust as an unreliable reflex meant only to keep humans from eating poison. Yip challenges that directly. He suggests this same gut reaction has adapted to flag social betrayal and moral pollution. Read alongside the biology of our squished-together brains, Yip’s argument feels like a surprising twist. The physical gag reflex stops looking like a simple relic of an ancient nervous system. Instead, a primitive gut reaction and a high-level moral compass start to look like the exact same thing.
