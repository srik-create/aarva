Sengupta shows how Chattopadhyay’s Rajani is trapped by her "low-born" status until the plot provides a convenient way out. It’s a nineteenth-century literary solution: the blind flower girl eventually gets to trade her basket for a mansion because she turns out to be a gentleman’s daughter after all. The novel fixes the social friction by rewriting the girl’s history. But look at the children in the next report from modern Assam. For twelve-year-old Nishanta Rabha, there’s no secret lineage waiting to be discovered. His father is a daily wager, and his reality is shaped by the search for work to help his family survive. The interesting thing is that while the old novel uses high-caste blood to grant its heroine a seat at the table, these students are looking at a classroom chair as the tool to build their own. They aren't dreaming of being rescued by a plot twist; they want to become the ones doing the rescuing—as teachers or local leaders. Where Chattopadhyay’s world requires a miracle of birth to solve poverty, the report from Goalpara suggests the only way out is through the "will" Nishanta mentions. It’s a shift from a story about who you were born as to a story about what you can learn to become.

A blind girl selling flowers in an old Bengali novel finds escape when a secret about her birth comes to light at the very end. That’s a tidy ending. In the real classrooms of Assam, students are building their own exits with a pen and a notebook. Fiction fixes a life with a single lucky stroke. Real change moves at the slow speed of a lesson plan. What lingers is this: if a person's worth isn't decided by their father's name anymore, who gets to decide when the work is finally enough?
