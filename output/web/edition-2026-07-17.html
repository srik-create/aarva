The Conversation ·Aldo Romero, Professor of Physics and Astronomy, West Virginia University

A flashlight beam cutting through a dark room follows a straight line, at least to the naked eye. At the atomic scale, however, light travels every possible path at once. Writing on 15 July 2026, a physicist explains why this strange behavior is moving from whiteboard theory into the hardware of new computers. These machines might eventually crack the codes protecting bank accounts and medical records, turning a spooky scientific quirk into a tool that could reshape the way everyone stays safe online.

Quantum mechanics relies on principles like superposition and entanglement, where particles exist in multiple states or remain linked across distances. These concepts form the basis for quantum computers, which use qubits to perform calculations that are impossible for standard machines. The technology has the potential to change drug discovery and digital encryption, though current hardware remains limited by the extreme fragility of quantum states.

If a person speaks harshly about someone, are they to blame when a stranger turns violent?

Against "Stochastic Terrorism"

Astral Codex Ten ·Scott Alexander

The murder of a health insurance CEO and threats against political figures have brought a heavy label back into the headlines: stochastic terrorism. Writing on 16 July 2026, the author looks at how this term is used by almost every side of the political map to quiet their critics. The tension isn't about whether words have power, but whether a free society can function if speakers are held responsible for the acts of every unstable person who hears them. It suggests the only stable path is a return to a simpler rule: criticize anyone, but never excuse the violence.

Stochastic terrorism is the idea that harsh public criticism makes a speaker responsible for later acts of violence against their target. This analysis tracks how the term is used across the political spectrum and tests several arguments for limiting what people are allowed to say. It argues for a return to the principle that individuals are solely responsible for their own crimes, regardless of the political speech that came before.
