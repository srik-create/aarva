Picture someone sitting alone, trying to make sense of a difficult book. Now picture a group of mathematicians scrambling to crack a decades-old problem before a machine beats them to it. A surprising thread connects these scenes. In Psyche, Celine Nguyen looks at why true education relies on a community of peers to stumble alongside, rather than solitary study. Taking a different angle, Ben Brubaker reports for Quanta Magazine on researchers racing against artificial intelligence, finding that the frustrating process of human failure is what actually makes discovery meaningful. Read together, these pieces suggest that the messy, social friction of getting things wrong matters just as much as finally arriving at the right answer.

Staring at YouTube lectures to learn philosophy usually ends in distraction. In an essay published today, Celine Nguyen argues that a real education relies on a community of peers instead of solitary study. Listen for the historical example she uses to make her case: 18th-century Scottish miners. By pooling their wages for books and holding mutual improvement debates, those workers proved that self-directed learning actually requires other people.

Celine Nguyen leaves a very specific image of what an education actually looks like: a group of 18th-century Scottish miners pooling money for books and talking through ideas together. It is messy, social, and entirely dependent on other people. Keep that image in mind reading Ben Brubaker. At first glance, a story about an MIT professor racing an artificial intelligence to solve a computational complexity problem seems like a completely different universe. But notice what happens when the threat of a machine solving the math problem looms over the human researchers. The AI might be able to spit out a flawless proof in an instant, but Brubaker captures the exact human element the machine misses. Dor Minzer and his graduate students scramble to write down their findings, rushing a 95-page draft online just to share their incomplete progress with their peers. Read alongside Nguyen, Minzer’s frantic scramble starts to look a lot like those Scottish miners. The final, perfect answer suddenly seems like only half the point. The real work of learning—whether someone is reading philosophy for the first time or trying to crack a decades-old math conjecture—is the deeply social act of figuring it out together.

What's left to sit with is the sound of chalk snapping against a board, or a student stumbling over a hard sentence. Solitary study has its place. Still, real learning usually happens out loud, next to someone else making the exact same mistakes. The sheer frustration of hitting a dead end is what makes the final answer mean something. If a computer can eventually hand over a flawless solution in seconds, what happens to the minds that no longer have to struggle to find it?
