A man who calls the rain looks up at a sky that no longer makes sense. It is a striking image of what happens when old traditions crack under modern pressures. Writing for The Independent, Rodney Muhumuza and Joseph Falzetta follow East African rainmakers who now face mob violence from desperate farmers as climate change scrambles the weather. Robert Philipson finds a similar breaking point in Africa Is a Country. He looks at the Swahili shoga—a man historically accepted into women's spaces—whose unique social role was slowly erased by British colonial laws and imported culture wars. Read together, these stories show how highly specific community figures end up crushed by massive outside forces.

In an essay published last week, Robert Philipson looks at the shoga—men on the East African coast who dressed as women and were accepted into women's society. Notice how Philipson traces the origin of the word itself. It was Swahili women who named them, using a term meaning "girlfriend" to welcome them into their domestic lives. Listen for how that quiet, centuries-old acceptance was eventually erased by European missionaries and Western categories.

Robert Philipson leaves you looking at the quiet domestic spaces of Swahili women, where a specific social role worked perfectly well until outside forces decided it was a problem. The shoga wasn't destroyed from within; they were erased by imported European judgments. Read alongside Philipson, the next piece by Rodney Muhumuza and Joseph Falzetta takes that same dynamic—a highly specific local figure crushed by massive global forces—and shifts it from culture to climate. Notice the difference in how the tradition breaks, though. Where Philipson writes about a role erased by colonial laws, Muhumuza and Falzetta follow East African rainmakers who are being hunted by their own neighbors. When farmers chase David Nabutsilili down a rocky hill and break his leg because too much water flooded their vegetable gardens, you are watching a localized punishment for a global crisis. The rainmaker is expected to control weather patterns that have been scrambled by a warming planet thousands of miles away. Put these two together, and you see how fragile local customs become when they collide with forces entirely outside their control—whether that force is a foreign moral code or a changing atmosphere.
