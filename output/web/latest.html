Be More Judgmental

The Hedgehog Review ·Hannah Katznelson

In an essay published on 28 October 2026, The Hedgehog Review asks why literature departments are losing their cultural footing right when readers need them most. As language models flood screens with text, people are desperate for someone to tell them what is actually worth their time. Yet academics increasingly refuse to judge literary quality, preferring to justify their work with vague claims about critical thinking. The piece suggests that to survive this era of endless content, scholars need to stop apologizing for their expertise and start doing what they were trained to do: sort good writing from bad.

An argument for using strict standards of literary quality to solve the modern crisis in the humanities. The author compares today's information age to the invention of the printing press, showing how early scholars managed a flood of new books by telling readers exactly what was worth their time. But a striking contrast emerges when placing that anxiety next to a dispatch published today by Sarah Waring. In Serbia, the response to a threatened democracy took a very different shape. Side by side, these accounts ask a sharp question about what happens when democratic systems crack. Do citizens try to patch the old institutions, or build new ones?

In the days leading up to the 2026 midterms, the federal government threatened to prosecute local officials who refused to reject certain ballots. Peter Elkind leaves you watching lawyers and poll workers scramble to patch up a system cracking under pressure. The focus stays entirely inside the existing machinery of American elections. Officials fight court battles and monitors watch the rolls, all trying to hold the line against institutional collapse. Turn to Sarah Waring’s account from Serbia, and the starting assumption completely shifts. Waring tracks what happens when citizens decide the official structures are already too corrupted to save from the inside. Instead of relying on state institutions, Serbian students and workers built their own parallel civic engine. Notice the sheer physical scale of the alternative infrastructure Waring describes. Eighty thousand volunteers organized their own mobile teams, food delivery networks, and anti-fraud units to bypass the regime's falsified voter registers. Read back-to-back, the contrast is sharp. The American defenders pour all their energy into protecting the official rules. Waring’s activists assume the rules are rigged, and find their power by organizing entirely outside them.

What lingers after these two accounts is a stark contrast in survival instincts. On one side, lawyers and clerks grip the rulebook tighter, trusting courtrooms to hold the line. On the other, students and workers simply walk away from rigged ballot boxes to build something new in the streets. Both are acts of defense, just fought with completely different tools. When the structure of a government starts to crack, how does a person know whether to stay and patch the foundation, or walk out the door?
