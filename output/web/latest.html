Katie Worth leaves you standing in a conference center in Turkey, watching seven hundred industry lobbyists strong-arm the United Nations into backing a carbon capture fantasy just to keep pumping oil. It feels like a story about raw, unstoppable power. But turn to Sue Biniaz, and the collapse in Antalya suddenly looks much more fragile. Biniaz points out that the entire summit didn't actually fail. The negotiations hit a wall over one highly specific, completely optional piece of paper called a cover decision. Put the two together, and the picture shifts. Worth shows you the fossil fuel industry throwing its weight around, but Biniaz shows you exactly where they aimed that weight. The lobbyists didn't have to break the whole global consensus to stall climate action. They just had to jam up the procedural ribbon tying the final package together. Read alongside Biniaz, the petrostate victory in Worth's reporting starts to look less like an overwhelming show of force, and more like a precise, bureaucratic sabotage.

What lingers is just how fragile the machinery of global survival actually is. A summit meant to cool the earth rarely collapses from a dramatic walkout. Instead, it gets quietly dismantled in the details. The rules designed to bring nations together end up providing the exact loopholes needed to run out the clock, leaving the door open for those who want to keep pumping oil. The question left hanging is this: if the current diplomatic system is so easily jammed by the people profiting from the present, what kind of system could actually force them to stop?
