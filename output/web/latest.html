Is everything conscious?

Aeon ·Sam Kimpton-Nye

When a dashboard warning light flashes, a driver expects a mechanical cause. When an asteroid leaves a massive crater, scientists look for an impact. But what happens when people demand that same perfect logic from human consciousness? Published on 12 October 2026, the essay follows that demand straight into a strange theory called panpsychism—the idea that basic awareness is baked into everything, from electrons to desk chairs. Ultimately, the argument pauses on a quieter question: why are humans so desperate to explain away the mysteries of the universe in the first place?

An examination of the philosophical principle that everything in the universe must have a logical explanation, and how this demand leads to panpsychism. Building on the ideas of Leibniz and Spinoza, the essay outlines three arguments for treating consciousness as a basic property of all physical matter. It concludes by questioning whether the desire for perfect explanations is actually a useful goal for philosophy.

But as a piece published this week in Smithsonian Magazine points out, astronomers at those South African dishes are currently trying to detect the magnetic fields of planets orbiting faraway suns. Down at the opposite pole, researchers featured in The Conversation are using Antarctic ice as a massive trap for cosmic particles and meteorites. Something interesting emerges when putting these two updates side by side. Catching faint signals from unreachable, invisible worlds depends almost entirely on the harshest, most isolated places on Earth.

Sixty-three light-years away, a massive planet called Beta Pictoris b is throwing off strange radio bursts. A piece published last week in Smithsonian Magazine tracks how astronomers are using those distant buzzes to map something completely invisible. What is worth listening for is the sheer scale of the discovery: a magnetic field 2,000 times stronger than Earth's. Notice how these radio waves do not just prove the field exists, but point to a violent, extreme version of our northern lights.
