Humans & Humanity

Why does choosing a playable character feel easier than figuring out how to actually live?

We Are All Playable Characters

Asterisk

People increasingly describe their daily routines in the language of video games: running side quests, dodging NPCs, and channeling main character energy. This 11 November 2026 essay from Asterisk asks what happens when society stops treating life as a novel and starts treating it as a playable game. While stories demand patience to see how an ending gives meaning to the past, games offer immediate instructions on what to do right now. The piece pauses to ask whether this shift makes life easier to manage, or just smaller.

A look at the shift from understanding human life as a story to treating it as a video game. While the traditional narrative model requires waiting for an ending to make sense of events, modern gaming concepts offer immediate direction through playable roles like the "tradwife" or "girlboss." Taking on these character types allows people to bypass the search for long-term identity, replacing it with a set of daily tasks and clear rules for behavior.

Reading Olayinka Stephen Ilesanmi, what jumps out is how stopping a deadly virus looks mostly like a social problem. His immune system naturally suppresses the virus, and scientists want to reverse-engineer his biology to create a functional cure. Read alongside Ilesanmi, Kwon’s focus on microscopic defenses feels like a completely different war. Ilesanmi tracks how a virus moves through a grieving village. What's left to sit with is the massive distance between a village meeting and a genetic sequencer. Survival against a deadly virus can depend on a quick wire transfer and a handshake with a local leader, just as much as it depends on mapping the silent, microscopic battles inside a single immune cell. Both fronts are entirely necessary to stop an outbreak. But looking at the gap between biological science and human behavior, a question lingers. When the next pathogen arrives, will the hardest hurdle be engineering the right cure, or convincing people to trust the hands delivering it?
