Is having faith the same kind of belief as knowing that a slab of concrete is hard?

Believing and thinking

Aeon ·Neil Van Leeuwen & Jacob Eisler

When Thomas Jefferson first proposed a wall between church and state, he treated religious convictions like any other opinion. Cognitive science suggests they are something else entirely. In an essay published on 3 November 2026, Neil Van Leeuwen and Jacob Eisler look at how the human brain processes faith compared to ordinary facts. A factual belief reacts to evidence, while religious faith operates as a voluntary, compartmentalized choice. That psychological distinction sets up a highly practical question. If faith and fact run on separate mental tracks, how should a government handle them?

An explanation of how the psychological differences between religious faith and factual belief justify the separation of church and state. Drawing on cognitive science and anthropology, the authors show that religious faith is voluntary, unaffected by evidence, and kept separate from everyday actions, while factual belief involuntarily responds to evidence. These mental differences explain why private faith can safely coexist with public, evidence-based democratic debate.

A report on recent experiments testing whether large language models can recognize their own internal states. Crosscut · banning the workaround

22 min ·narrated by Sulafat/Charon/Vindemiatrix

Without a police force to call, a victim of theft in 17th-century England often hired a "service magician" to track down the culprit. A piece in Smithsonian Magazine shows how magistrates hunted down these magical private eyes, banning the informal workaround long before building a real alternative. Writing in Africa Is a Country, Fikayo Akeredolu spots a similar dynamic in modern Nigeria. The government just eliminated a petrol subsidy citizens relied on to run personal generators, while the national power grid remains broken. Putting these two situations side by side raises a question: why do governments across centuries share a habit of destroying the messy systems people use to get by?

In 1642, a weaver’s wife used a sieve and a pair of shears to track down a stolen purse. Before police forces existed, people hired these local "service magicians" to solve petty crimes. A piece published this week in Smithsonian Magazine looks at how the English city of Exeter hunted down these everyday sleuths, long after the rest of the country stopped prosecuting witches. Notice the incredibly ordinary household tools these magicians used to name their suspects.

The piece on Exeter leaves you picturing a city aggressively hunting down the local women who helped neighbors find stolen shillings. The authorities cracked down on these informal detectives long before anyone had invented an actual police force to do the job instead. Fast forward four hundred years, and Fikayo Akeredolu looks at a completely different kind of government reform in modern Nigeria. At first glance, cutting a costly fossil fuel subsidy has nothing to do with seventeenth-century magic. But read alongside that history of Exeter, Akeredolu’s argument about electricity starts to look incredibly familiar. He points out that cheap petrol is the only way millions of Nigerians can run their home generators, because the national power grid barely works. When the state removes the subsidy to please international economists, it does exactly what those English judges did. It destroys the messy, imperfect workaround regular people rely on to get by, long before it bothers to build a working system to replace it.

A stolen sheep in the 1600s or a dark house in Lagos today leaves a person with the same immediate problem. When the official system is broken, people find a back door. They hire a local wizard. They buy cheap fuel for a noisy generator. The state eventually steps in to shut those back doors down, demanding order. What lingers is the gap between tearing down a makeshift solution and building a real one. When authorities outlaw the rough tools people use to survive, what exactly are citizens supposed to do while they wait for the official fix?
