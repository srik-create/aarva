Where is the line between a machine spotting a pothole and a machine judging a life?

The promise and peril of AI in government

Rest of World ·Beth Simone Noveck

When a city uses artificial intelligence to spot a pothole, it is measuring something that already exists. But when a judge uses it to guess if a defendant will commit another crime, the technology becomes something else entirely. Writing on 12 November 2026, Beth Simone Noveck draws a hard line between these two uses of automation. She asks why so many institutions are eager to hand over life-altering human decisions to software they cannot completely understand.

An assessment of artificial intelligence in government services that draws a line between pattern recognition and predictive algorithms. Using city programs in Taoyuan, Taiwan as a baseline, it outlines five ways predictive models fail when applied to individual human behavior. It argues that while machine learning can safely identify existing problems like tumors or pollution, using it to forecast job performance or criminal activity often results in automated discrimination.

When an army moves, the hardest part isn't the fighting. It's hauling the fuel, parts, and bullets needed to keep troops alive. Keeping those supply lines running is a quiet, massive crisis, and two recent pieces approach it from opposite ends of the telescope. Writing in late October, Luis Márquez turns to the sprawling factories at home, asking how better data could force traditional weapons makers to speed up production. But a piece published today by Lucas Fluegel abandons those long, vulnerable routes entirely. He looks to synthetic biology, wondering what happens if the military packs microscopic factories into shipping containers—using engineered microbes to grow fuel and materials right on the front lines.

Writing in late October, Luis Márquez points out a blind spot in how military planners count ships and missiles. The real bottleneck in a Taiwan crisis is how fast defense factories can actually build replacements. Listen for the stark contrast he draws between the two powers. While American manufacturers are still geared for peacetime production, China has already built its industrial base to run under wartime conditions.

Reading Luis Márquez leaves you staring at the factory floor. He points to massive shipyards and defense plants, arguing that victory in a place like Taiwan depends on squeezing more missiles out of assembly lines thousands of miles away. Turn the page to Lucas Fluegel, and that entire industrial mindset gets turned inside out. Fluegel shares the exact same anxiety about running dry in a crisis. But instead of trying to optimize the giant facilities back home, he looks at the fragile cargo ships trying to carry those goods across a contested ocean. Notice how he shifts the focus from the home front to the battlefield. His solution is to bypass those vulnerable supply chains entirely by packing microscopic factories—engineered microbes that can grow fuel and plastics—into shipping containers right at the edge of the combat zone. Read alongside Márquez, Fluegel's proposal stops sounding like science fiction and starts looking like a basic survival tactic. The two writers approach the same logistics nightmare from opposite ends. One wants to fix the giant machine at home; the other wants to grow a new one on the spot.

What lingers is the sheer distance between a steel factory in Ohio and a vat of bacteria on the front lines. One approach tries to fix the old industrial machine, hoping better spreadsheets can force those massive plants to move faster. The other abandons the supply chain entirely, betting on engineered microbes to brew fuel right in a combat zone. It forces a choice about where to place trust when things break down. If the cargo ships stop moving tomorrow, does survival depend on a perfectly tuned assembly line back home, or a living organism multiplying in the dirt?
