Could making the clouds a little brighter stop a super El Niño before it starts?

Could We Mitigate Super El Niños by Artificially Changing the Climate? A New Study Indicates Yes

Smithsonian Magazine

The Pacific Ocean is heating up, and with it, the threat of a super El Niño that could scramble the weather across the globe. A 9 July 2026 report in Smithsonian Magazine looks at a study that suggests cooling things down by spraying salt into the sky. This method, known as marine cloud brightening, turns clouds into mirrors to bounce sunlight back into space. It offers a shift in how to think about fixing the climate: not as a permanent overhaul, but as a temporary fix to stop a specific disaster before it begins.

A study using computer models suggests that making clouds more reflective could stop the most extreme El Niño weather events. Researchers simulated adding particles to clouds over the Pacific Ocean to block sunlight, a process that turned past super El Niños into neutral weather patterns. The findings show how targeted climate engineering might prevent natural disasters, though the technology for such an effort does not yet exist.

How did a game meant to exhaust rowdy schoolboys turn into the world’s most popular sport?

Far removed from today’s global juggernaut, soccer was born in the well-heeled boarding schools of 19th-century England

The Conversation ·Thomas Adam, Professor of Political Science, University of Arkansas

The bright lights of modern soccer are a long way from the rowdy hallways of 19th-century English boarding schools. In this 29 May 2026 piece, the game’s origins appear not as a passion for sport, but as a tactical move to manage spoiled, rebellious teenagers. By forcing students to chase a ball across a meadow, teachers hoped to drain the energy that usually went into schoolhouse riots. It turns out the world’s most popular game started as a Victorian experiment in classroom management.

Soccer began in 19th-century English boarding schools as a way for teachers to control rowdy students. The sport grew from violent local traditions into a game with set rules, eventually splitting between rugby and soccer. These school games led to the first professional leagues and tournaments.
