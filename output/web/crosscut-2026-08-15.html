Finding an unknown creature usually conjures images of dusty bones pulled from the earth. But the frontier of biological discovery has shifted away from physical fossils and into the genetic code of organisms alive right now. A recent piece in Smithsonian Magazine shows researchers skipping the archaeological dig to find extinct ancestors. Instead, they are tracing ancient "ghost" populations by reading the DNA of living people. Meanwhile, Benji Jones writes in Vox about scientists scanning the genetic barcodes of insects buzzing around New York City to identify entirely new species. Putting these two approaches together reveals something surprising. The most mysterious creatures aren't always buried in ancient rock. Sometimes, they are hiding in plain sight.

Published just last week in Smithsonian Magazine, this piece looks at how scientists are tracking down extinct human ancestors without digging up a single bone. Instead of relying on rare fossils, researchers are mapping our family tree using the DNA of people walking around today. Reading the genetic code of 500 living people, the Smithsonian piece leaves you with a strange image: the ghosts of extinct ancestors are still walking around inside the modern human body. You don't necessarily need to dig up a million-year-old bone to find a lost branch of the family tree. That shift—from digging in the dirt to reading the genes of the living—is exactly where Benji Jones picks up the thread. But instead of looking backward at human history, Jones looks outward at the modern city. He sets up a five-foot bug trap right in the middle of New York, catching thousands of flies and wasps. By reading their DNA barcodes, he realizes that entirely unknown species are likely buzzing around Central Park right now. Read alongside the Smithsonian article, Jones’s urban experiment changes how you might picture a biological frontier. The undiscovered world is simply hiding in plain sight, written into the DNA of the people waiting on a subway platform, and the insects flying past the streetlamps above them.
