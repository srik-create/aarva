When India and China look across the border, why do they only see old wounds?

The Real Problem Underlying India-China Tensions

Noema ·Pallavi Aiyar

For the past few years, India and China have essentially banned each other’s journalists. The two countries share a massive border and a third of the global population, yet they rely almost entirely on Western reporters to translate their neighbor's actions. Writing on 23 July 2026, Pallavi Aiyar frames this media vacuum as the symptom of a much older problem. Beyond the troop standoffs and economic rivalry, Beijing and New Delhi are trapped by century-old grievances. When their diplomats sit down to talk, they are usually having completely different conversations, unable to read the other's basic intentions.

An examination of the historical memories driving the tensions between India and China. Traces Chinese skepticism of India to colonial-era Sikh policemen and opium merchants, and Indian distrust of China to the 1962 border war. Concludes with a case for bringing back resident foreign correspondents so the two countries can observe each other directly.

How does medicine change when a drug is just a sequence of genetic code?

A future of programmable medicine

Works in Progress ·Works in Progress

For decades, inventing a new drug has meant relying on endless trial and error to find a chemical shape that happens to block a disease. Writing on 26 August 2026, Jacob Witten looks at a shift away from this guessing game. The piece asks what happens when medicine stops being a chemistry problem and becomes a programming one. If a disease is driven by a specific gene, new therapies mean scientists no longer have to search for a chemical cure. They can just write a sequence of code to switch the problem off.

An overview of how small interfering RNA is turning drug development from chemical trial-and-error into a programmable process of gene silencing. Outlines the decades-long engineering effort to keep these fragile molecules intact long enough to enter human cells. Surveys the current state of these therapies, moving from approved treatments for liver conditions to new clinical trials aimed at the brain.

If the actual work of governing is boring, why do politicians have to be exciting?

Politics should be more boring

Slow Boring ·Matthew Yglesias

A 27 August 2026 piece from Slow Boring observes a strange habit in modern politics. Primary voters and cable news consistently reward candidates who know how to go viral and dominate a news cycle. But the day-to-day work of running a country involves tedious technical tasks, like balancing budgets and drafting policy. That leaves a practical question: if being an entertaining attention-grabber fails to help a leader win a general election or govern effectively, why does the political system demand it?

An argument against the modern demand for exciting, attention-grabbing politicians. By contrasting viral candidates with low-key but successful governors, the analysis separates the skills needed to win a crowded primary from those required to win a general election. Argues that voters should prioritize managerial competence over entertainment.
