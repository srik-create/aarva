Inside American statehouses today, lobbyists are pushing for new laws against "Hinduphobia." A century ago in Tokyo, imperial officials spread quiet rumors that the Japanese emperor had converted to Islam. Two writers show how these vastly different moments share a strategy: manufacturing a religious narrative to gain power. Raju Rajagopal tracks how modern Hindu nationalist groups use claims of religious persecution to shield themselves from political criticism in the United States. Looking backward, Mimi Hanaoka uncovers Japan's early twentieth-century campaign to court Muslim allies and build an anti-Western coalition across Asia. Placing these histories together reveals something striking about how easily faith is put to work when a political movement needs to secure its flanks.

In an essay published this week, Raju Rajagopal looks at how Hindu nationalist groups in the US use laws against "Hinduphobia" to shield themselves from political criticism. Pay attention to the specific local tensions he describes in Frisco, Texas. He points out that as the town's Indian population has grown, the resulting hostility from the American right looks remarkably similar to the anti-Muslim hate spread by Hindu supremacists.

Raju Rajagopal leaves you watching a very modern political fight in the Texas suburbs. In town council meetings and statehouses, organizations are trying to engineer a specific religious narrative—loud claims of "Hinduphobia"—to carve out power and deflect criticism. It looks like a localized response to the current American immigration debate. Mimi Hanaoka turns the page to a completely different century and continent, but she exposes a surprisingly similar playbook. Her historical account looks at imperial Japan in the early 1900s. Instead of using religious identity as a defensive shield, the Japanese government used it as bait. They let a wild rumor fly that their emperor was converting to Islam, all to court Muslim allies across Asia and build an anti-Western coalition. Notice what surfaces when you put these two strategies side by side. The shared assumption is that religious identity is just another tool to be manipulated for influence. Rajagopal’s account of Texas lobbying feels highly specific to modern conservative politics. But read alongside Hanaoka, those contemporary claims of religious persecution start to look less like a spontaneous reaction to local bigotry, and more like a century-old strategy of using a sacred identity for a calculated, secular advantage.

What lingers here is how easily the sacred gets turned into a shield or a recruitment poster. A whispered rumor about an emperor in Tokyo and a drafted bill in an American statehouse both do the exact same work. They dress raw political ambition in the untouchable language of faith, daring anyone to object. It leaves a quiet, difficult question behind. When political organizers start using a religion just to build empires or deflect criticism, how do everyday believers protect their faith from the people claiming to defend it?
