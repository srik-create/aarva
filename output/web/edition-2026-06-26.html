Could the homes a city needs be built by the very people it once forced out?

How the Squamish built Senakw

Works in Progress ·Works in Progress

Writing on 11 June 2026, Anya Martin looks at a rare exception to the housing gridlock that defines so many modern cities. In Vancouver, the Squamish Nation is using reclaimed land to build eleven massive towers, bypassing the local planning rules that usually stall such projects for decades. The story moves past the history of dispossession to look at a practical reality: when a community directly captures the value of new housing, the political will to build suddenly appears. It offers a glimpse into what urban growth looks like when the incentives finally align.

The Squamish Nation is constructing Senakw, a high-density residential development on returned ancestral land in Vancouver. By utilizing the site’s status as a federal reserve to bypass municipal zoning restrictions, the project aims to generate billions in revenue while addressing the city’s housing shortage. The history details the community’s 1913 forced removal and the legal and anthropological efforts that restored the land a century later.

How does the search for dark matter change when a clock is built from an atom's nucleus?

The World's First Nuclear Clocks Are Ticking, Opening a New Way to Investigate Dark Matter and Other Mysteries of Physics

Smithsonian Magazine

Writing on 24 June 2026, Smithsonian Magazine follows a quiet shift in how time is measured. For decades, the gold standard has been the atomic clock, which relies on the movement of electrons. But two independent teams have now successfully built clocks that tap into the nucleus of an atom instead. This transition from the shell to the nucleus isn't just about better precision; it suggests a future where timekeeping devices act as sensors for dark matter and other hidden forces that current technology simply cannot feel.

The development of the first functional nuclear clocks, which utilize thorium-229 nuclei excited by ultraviolet lasers to measure time. The research compares these new devices to traditional atomic clocks, detailing how nuclear transitions provide a frequency less affected by external electromagnetic fields. It examines the current precision of these prototypes and their potential utility in detecting dark matter and investigating fundamental physical constants.

What do a few fossil fish in the desert say about the birth of the modern ocean?

Fossil fishes buried in the desert reveal a missing chapter in marine history

The Conversation ·Sanaa El-Sayed, PhD student, Department of Earth and Environmental Sciences and Museum of Paleontology, University of Michigan

The piece, published on 4 June 2026, pauses on a persistent silence in the fossil record: the state of the oceans in the immediate wake of the asteroid that ended the dinosaurs. While the story of life on land is well-mapped, the marine world during those first few million years has remained largely unknown. A discovery in the Egyptian desert suggests the foundations of modern marine life were laid much earlier than previously understood. It is a quiet observation of how life finds its footing after a total collapse.

An account of the discovery and excavation of a fossil site in Egypt’s eastern desert dating to four million years after the end-Cretaceous mass extinction. Through fieldwork and laboratory analysis of nearly 500 specimens, the research identifies early relatives of modern lineages like tunas and pipefishes. The findings document the transition from Cretaceous-era marine life to the precursors of contemporary ocean ecosystems.
