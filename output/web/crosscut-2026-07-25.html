A daughter’s smile is often treated as a mere evolutionary defect by the Silicon Valley elites William Thibeau critiques for Compact, writing just two days ago. He tracks how billion-dollar firms like Anthropic are built on Effective Altruism, a philosophy that values abstract strangers over neighbors. Thibeau leaves the listener with the image of a spreadsheet trying to outvote a soul. In that world, a daughter’s smile is just one more data point to be weighed against the needs of a thousand strangers. It is a vision of morality where the "calculating reformer" wins by being perfectly logical and completely detached. But notice what happens when that cold math hits the actual friction of history. Sarah Setlaelo brings in Nelson Mandela, a leader who didn't work with a calculator or even a grand theory, but with what she calls pragmatism. Where Thibeau warns about the "bureaucrat" mask, Setlaelo describes a "philosopher king" who understood that real change doesn't come from a wealth pledge or an IPO. It comes from the "Janusian contradiction"—the difficult, face-to-face work of holding a divided country together. Read together, the two pieces suggest that the further a person gets from the neighbors right in front of them, the easier it is to mistake a formula for a virtue. Mandela’s legacy looks less like a set of rules and more like an argument that the most important moral work happens in the exact spots where the math fails.

What lingers is the gap between a data point and a handshake. A spreadsheet might show how to save lives in the next century, but it doesn't explain how to sit across from an enemy and find a way forward. Nelson Mandela didn't find peace in a formula; he found it in stubborn compromise. When the focus stays fixed on a distant future, the neighbors right here can feel like a distraction. At what point does the math of doing good become an excuse to avoid the harder work of being good to the person in the room?
