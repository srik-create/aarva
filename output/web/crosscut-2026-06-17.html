Crosscut · football and national identity

19 min ·narrated by Sulafat/Charon/Vindemiatrix

It’s worth pausing on how a football jersey is rarely just polyester and ink; it’s a claim to history. Writing this week, Julia Gaffield looks at why FIFA blocked Haiti from wearing a kit featuring symbols of its 1804 revolution. It feels like a modern echo of a long-standing effort to quiet Haiti’s past. Also writing this week, Alasdair Howorth examines a different kind of national expression through Cabo Verde’s philosophy of morabeza. While one nation faces institutional silence, the other uses the pitch to gather a diaspora under a specific cultural code. There’s a tension here between what global governors allow a team to say and how a small island nation chooses to define itself.

FIFA’s recent ban on a Haitian soccer jersey featuring the Battle of Vertières might look like a simple rules violation. But writing two days ago, Julia Gaffield, Professor of History, William & Mary, suggests the move fits a much older pattern of global censorship. Notice how she links this modern bureaucratic veto to the historical smear campaigns against Jean-Jacques Dessalines, the leader who dared to declare Haiti’s independence.

Julia Gaffield illustrates how a global bureaucracy like FIFA can effectively erase a nation’s foundational memory by labeling its origin story "political." The image of Jean-Jacques Dessalines and the Battle of Vertières is treated as a violation rather than a birth certificate, suggesting that for some, the price of entering the global arena is a mandatory historical amnesia. Turning to Alasdair Howorth’s account of Cabo Verde, the dynamic shifts from external policing to internal willpower. While Haiti is forced to scrub its symbols, Cabo Verde leans into its own cultural philosophy of morabeza. The interesting thing is that both pieces treat the football pitch as a site for processing the scars of slavery and colonial neglect. Read alongside Gaffield, the "No Stress" motto Howorth describes starts to look like more than a team-building exercise. It appears as a successful attempt to package a history of hardship—specifically the survival of Portuguese abandonment—into a performance of identity that the world actually accepts. Where Haiti’s revolutionary symbols are seen as a threat to the game’s neutrality, Cabo Verde’s philosophy is embraced as the secret to its success, revealing a lopsided boundary for which national stories are allowed to be told out loud.

What lingers is the friction between a nation’s private memory and its public uniform. While Haiti’s revolutionary symbols were scrubbed from the fabric by distant officials, Cabo Verde’s morabeza was carried in the players' quiet hospitality. One story was silenced by a rulebook; the other was sustained by a shared spirit. These moments suggest a match is rarely just about the score, but about the right to exist out loud. When a global stage demands a sanitized version of history, what parts of a people’s soul are left behind in the locker room?
