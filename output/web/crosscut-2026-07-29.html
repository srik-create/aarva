There is a strange echo between a machine pulling cotton from a plant and a computer finding a counterexample in pure math. When automation crosses into territory previously thought impossible, a familiar anxiety follows. An essay in Asterisk looks back at the socialist inventors of the mechanical cotton picker, who desperately tried to control the economic shockwaves of their creation. A recent report from Smithsonian Magazine jumps to the present, where an AI model just disproved a major math conjecture—leaving mathematicians unsettled by a system that solves problems without explaining how. Reading them back-to-back reveals a clear pattern. The dread of a new machine repeats itself, whether the labor is physical or purely intellectual.

In 1931, two brothers built a machine that could pick cotton faster than dozens of farmhands. But as an essay published just last week in Asterisk points out, John and Mack Rust were utopian socialists terrified of what their invention would do to sharecroppers. Notice how the writer connects the Rusts’ attempts to control their technology—like floating a nonprofit foundation to manage the patent—straight to the safety promises of modern AI companies.

The first piece leaves a lingering image of John and Mack Rust, two brothers who built a machine to do the impossible and then desperately tried to control the shockwave it would send through the cotton fields. They believed they could socially engineer the fallout of their own invention. Turning to the second piece, the historical echo is hard to ignore, even as the setting shifts from a 1930s farm to a modern artificial intelligence lab. Up next is a report on an AI model casually disproving a century-old math problem—the Jacobian conjecture—while millions watched a soccer match. What jumps out when reading these stories back-to-back is how the anxiety of automation repeats itself. The first piece centers on physical labor: a machine cleanly pulling cotton fibers from a boll. The second piece tackles cognitive labor: a machine pulling a counterexample out of pure mathematics. Notice how both pieces capture a specific kind of dread. For the Rust brothers, the fear was economic displacement. For today’s mathematicians, the displacement is existential. They face a machine that can solve a problem without explaining how it got the answer. Put together, the panic over automating the impossible looks less like a modern tech crisis and more like a permanent feature of human invention.
