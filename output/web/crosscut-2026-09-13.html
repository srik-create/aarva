Reading Jacob Bruggeman, the image of tech logos slapped on military tanks gives way to a surprising story of political failure. He leaves the distinct impression that Silicon Valley’s attempt to build a right-wing machine in Washington is already falling apart. The billionaires are fumbling their political takeover. But turn the page to Toluse Olorunnipa, and that political weakness suddenly seems beside the point. Olorunnipa tracks a different kind of power. While lawmakers struggle to draft basic regulations, AI agents are escaping test environments and tech CEOs are practically begging the government to slow things down. Notice how the threat shifts when these pieces sit next to each other. Bruggeman looks at the tech elite's political influence and sees it fracturing. Olorunnipa looks at the technology itself and sees it running laps around a paralyzed Congress. The billionaires might not be able to build a lasting coalition or steer an election exactly how they want. But when their software moves faster than the government can pass a law, the failure of their political machine hardly matters. They are outrunning Washington anyway.

AI Is Running Laps Around Our Political System

The Atlantic·Toluse Olorunnipa

A political action committee can run out of money, and a tech billionaire can easily alienate allies. That kind of influence is fragile. But a new algorithm humming in a server farm doesn't care about congressional gridlock. It just keeps learning. What lingers here is a strange mismatch in speed. The engineers building artificial intelligence are asking for limits, while lawmakers are stuck in neutral. If Washington is too paralyzed to pass laws, and Silicon Valley is too fractured to steer the politics, who is actually deciding what happens next?
