Eight thousand school staff members could lose their jobs to pay for their own colleagues' raises. Writing for The Independent earlier this month, Ellie Ng looks at why teachers in England are threatening to strike over a 3.5 percent pay increase. What’s worth listening for is how the math breaks down in a single constituency like Makerfield, where dozens of schools are being forced to find nearly a million pounds in their already stretched budgets.

The image of 8,300 teachers and support staff disappearing from English classrooms just to balance a ledger leaves a heavy question about what, exactly, is being bought and sold in a school budget. Ng shows a moment where the work of educating children is treated as an interchangeable expense—a line on a spreadsheet that can be trimmed to make the math work. But look at Joshua Habgood-Coote’s history of skill nostalgia and a different pattern emerges. He traces the loss of the 18th-century silk weavers’ flower gardens as they were pulled into factories, suggesting that the modern craving for handmade goods is actually a protest against being treated like a machine. Read alongside the teacher strike, this nostalgia looks less like a hobbyist’s whim and more like a defensive crouch. If Ng shows the struggle to fund a profession, Habgood-Coote shows the older, deeper fear of losing the craft of work entirely. The two pieces suggest that whether it’s a weaver losing his Monday recreation or a teacher losing a classroom assistant, the underlying fight is the same: a resistance to the idea that human skill is just another input to be optimized.

A classroom in Birmingham or a dusty woodshop from a century ago both hold the same tension. It’s the friction between the person doing the work and the ledger trying to simplify it. When a teacher is forced to trade a colleague for a paycheck, the job stops being a calling and starts looking like a factory floor. What’s left to sit with is the stubborn refusal to be treated like a line item. If a job is eventually stripped down to its cheapest parts, what is left to sustain the person holding the chalk or the chisel?
